Attention Is All You Need

Mapping the Venture Ecosystem Through Follow Graph Analysis

Thomas Shehadeh • October 2026

"The map is not the territory — but a good map reveals structure the territory hides."

§1. On Attention

There is a moment — perhaps you have felt it — when you first see a map of something you thought you understood, and realize you understood nothing at all. The first time someone shows you a network graph of the internet, or the neural pathways of a brain, or the trade routes of ancient civilizations, something shifts. You see that beneath the chaos of individual events lies structure. Patterns. The ghost of an organizing principle that nobody designed but everybody participates in.

This document describes an attempt to see one such pattern: the network of attention that flows through the technology ecosystem. Specifically, we ask: who are the smartest people in venture capital paying attention to?

It sounds almost trivially simple. But consider: a venture capitalist's job is, fundamentally, to allocate attention before allocating capital. To notice what others have not yet noticed. To see the founder who will define the next decade while they are still working from a garage, or a dorm room, or a corner of someone else's office. The follow button on Twitter is, in a sense, the smallest possible unit of this attention — a micro-investment that costs nothing but reveals everything.

When one sophisticated investor follows an unknown account, it could be noise — a friend, a curiosity, a mistake. But when five of them do, independently, within the same month? That convergence contains information. It is the network speaking in a language we can learn to read.

§2. The Premise

The premise of this system is simple, and perhaps heretical: attention precedes capital, and capital precedes impact. If we can map attention flows in real-time, we can see where capital will flow next. And if we can see where capital will flow, we can see — however dimly — the shape of the future.

This is not prediction in the mystical sense. We are not reading tea leaves or consulting oracles. We are observing a leading indicator. The same way that certain birds fly south before anyone feels the cold, certain patterns of attention emerge before anyone reads the TechCrunch headline. The birds are not prophets; they are simply sensitive to signals we have learned to ignore.

The question is whether we can become sensitive again.

§3. The Network as Organism

To understand what we are building, it helps to think of the technology ecosystem not as a market, not as an industry, but as something closer to an organism. A vast, distributed intelligence made up of thousands of individual actors, each with their own goals and information, but connected by an invisible web of relationships, conversations, and — yes — follows.

This organism has properties that no individual possesses. It knows things that nobody knows. It makes decisions that nobody made. It allocates resources with a strange efficiency that emerges from chaos. Economists have a word for this: emergence. The spontaneous appearance of complex behavior from simple rules.

The follow graph is one window into this emergent intelligence. Not the only window — there are others: job changes, conference attendance, patent filings, academic citations. But the follow graph has two advantages: it updates in real-time, and it reveals attention rather than commitment. You can follow someone without telling anyone. It is the purest signal we have of what sophisticated actors are paying attention to.

§4. Architecture

The system comprises three layers, each building on the last:

4.1 The Sensing Layer — We maintain a set of approximately 600 "core nodes": venture capitalists, angels, operators, and scouts whose judgment we trust. These are the sensory neurons of our system. Each night, at midnight Pacific time, we query who each core node follows, comparing against yesterday's snapshot to detect new edges in the graph.

4.2 The Graph Layer — These follow events accumulate into a social graph G = (V, E), where vertices V represent Twitter accounts and edges E represent follow relationships. The graph currently contains 0 nodes and 0 edges. This is our representation of the ecosystem's attention structure.

4.3 The Intelligence Layer — Raw graphs are noise. To extract signal, we apply algorithms that have proven useful across domains from web search to social network analysis:

§5. PageRank: Recursive Importance

In 1998, Larry Page and Sergey Brin published a paper called "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine." In it, they described an algorithm for measuring the importance of web pages based on who links to them. The key insight was recursive: a page is important if important pages link to it. This circular definition, implemented as an iterative algorithm, became the foundation of Google.

We apply the same logic to the follow graph. An account is influential if influential accounts follow it. Being followed by @pmarca contributes more to your score than being followed by someone @pmarca has never heard of. Influence propagates through the network like heat through a metal.

The result is a ranking that differs from raw follower counts in important ways. A founder with 2,000 followers but followed by 15 top-tier VCs will rank higher than a crypto influencer with 200,000 followers but no edges to the investor subgraph. PageRank sees through vanity metrics to reveal structural importance.

§6. Community Detection: Finding Tribes

Networks are not uniform. They have structure — clusters, communities, tribes. People in AI follow other people in AI. Climate tech founders follow other climate tech founders. These clusters emerge not because anyone designed them, but because attention flows preferentially along lines of shared interest.

The Louvain algorithm (and its faster cousin, Leiden) detect these communities by maximizing a quantity called modularity: the degree to which the network divides into groups with dense internal connections and sparse external ones. The algorithm knows nothing about AI or climate or defense — it sees only edges. Yet the clusters it finds correspond remarkably well to the sectors we recognize.

§7. Momentum: The Derivative of Attention

Position matters, but velocity matters more. An account with moderate PageRank but rapidly increasing attention is more interesting than an established account with stable high PageRank. The former is becoming something. The latter already is.

We track momentum by computing the rate of change in key metrics: new follows from core nodes, change in PageRank, velocity of community integration. High momentum often precedes public events — funding announcements, product launches, viral moments. The network knows before the news cycle.

§8. On Seeing What Others Cannot

There is a particular kind of advantage that comes not from knowing secret information, but from seeing public information more clearly than others. The follow graph is public. Anyone can look. But almost nobody does — not systematically, not algorithmically, not with the patience to let patterns emerge from noise.

This is the opportunity. Not to know things others don't know, but to see things others don't see. To map a territory that everyone inhabits but nobody has charted. To turn the firehose of social data into a legible landscape.

Consider: every day, hundreds of interesting people are followed by sophisticated investors. These events are public. They leave traces. But who is watching? Who is aggregating? Who is asking: what does it mean when 7 different firms all follow the same nuclear physicist in the same week?

§9. Use Cases

The system enables several workflows that would be impossible manually:

9.1 Founder Discovery. Query the network for accounts matching specific criteria — "nuclear AND engineer AND NOT investor" — ranked by PageRank. Find founders before they're findable. Reach out when your email is one of three, not one of three hundred.

9.2 Sector Mapping. Identify everyone working in a space by examining who the known players follow. If you have 10 known defense tech founders, their collective follow graph reveals 100 more you've never heard of.

9.3 Trend Detection. Monitor which clusters are gaining attention fastest. When AI investors suddenly start following nuclear accounts, that's a signal. The network is repositioning before the thesis is public.

9.4 Competitive Intelligence. Track which founders your competitors are following. Map their attention before you're competing for the same deals.

"nuclear" → Returns founders/operators with nuclear, fusion, fission in bio, ranked by PageRank
"defense" + Last 30 days → Defense-related accounts that gained the most new VC followers this month
"AI safety" + Momentum → AI safety researchers sorted by follower velocity (day-over-day growth)
"@pmarca AND @naval" → Accounts followed by both, ranked by combined influence — finding shared conviction

§10. The Ethos

There is a philosophy embedded in this project, and it is worth making explicit.

First: technology should augment human judgment, not replace it. This system does not tell you who to invest in or who to work with. It tells you who to look at. The final judgment remains human — and must.

Second: networks are more honest than narratives. People say many things about what they believe, what they're building, what they're interested in. But the follow graph reveals revealed preference — what they actually pay attention to, stripped of performance and posturing. There is a truth in the graph that press releases cannot contain.

Third: the edges matter more than the nodes. We are trained to think in terms of individuals: this person is important, that person is influential. But in network analysis, the connections between people are often more informative than the people themselves. A founder is defined not just by who they are, but by who notices them, who validates them, who is willing to publicly associate their attention with them.

Fourth: emergence should be respected, not designed. The clusters we find are not categories we invented. They emerged from the data. This humility — this willingness to let the network tell us what it contains, rather than imposing our preconceptions — is essential to seeing clearly.

§12. The Core Finding

PageRank and follower count measure different things. The Spearman correlation between them is ρ = 0.22 — barely correlated. An account's popularity with the general public tells you almost nothing about its structural importance in the investor network.

By contrast, PageRank and VC follow count correlate at ρ = 0.74. This makes sense: both measure attention from sophisticated actors. But PageRank adds recursive weighting — being followed by @pmarca counts more than being followed by someone @pmarca has never heard of.

§16. Limitations

Selection bias. We track ~3,400 investors, mostly US-based, mostly tech-focused. The graph reflects their attention, not the world's. An important founder in biotech or deep tech may be invisible if our tracked investors haven't noticed them yet.

Survivorship bias. We see who investors follow today. We don't see who they unfollowed, or who they chose not to follow. The graph overstates consensus and understates disagreement.

Correlation ≠ causation. High PageRank doesn't cause funding. It correlates with attention from people who make funding decisions. An account could have high PageRank for reasons unrelated to fundability (entertainment value, controversy, personal friendship).

Twitter ≠ reality. Some important founders don't use Twitter. Some investors follow people ironically. The map is not the territory; it's a projection of the territory onto a particular medium.

§17. The Math

For completeness, the algorithms used:

PageRank computes the stationary distribution of a random walk on the graph. The score of node i is:

PR(i) = (1-d)/N + d × Σ PR(j)/L(j)

Where d = 0.85 (damping factor), N = total nodes, L(j) = out-degree of j, and the sum is over all j linking to i. We iterate until convergence (Δ < 10⁻⁶).

Louvain clustering maximizes modularity Q:

Q = (1/2m) × Σ [A_ij - (k_i × k_j)/(2m)] × δ(c_i, c_j)

Where m = total edges, A_ij = adjacency, k_i = degree of i, and δ = 1 if i and j are in the same community. Our modularity score of 0.256 indicates moderate community structure.

§22. Roadmap

The system as it exists today is a foundation. Several enhancements are planned:

  • LLM Integration — Analyze recent tweets + bio to auto-classify accounts (founder vs operator vs investor) and extract interests, investment thesis, and current priorities. The model reads; we search.
  • Real-time Alerts — Notify when 3+ core nodes follow the same account within 24 hours. Convergence of attention is signal; speed is competitive advantage.
  • Predictive Scoring — Identify accounts likely to raise funding in next 90 days based on attention patterns. The network knows before the press release.
  • Graph Neural Networks — Move beyond PageRank to learned embeddings. Let the network teach us features we haven't thought to look for.
  • Multi-platform Integration — Extend sensing beyond Twitter to LinkedIn, GitHub, conference attendance, academic citations. The more signals, the clearer the picture.

§23. Closing Thoughts

We began with a simple observation: attention is signal. Where sophisticated actors pay attention, information exists. Where attention converges, alpha hides.

The technology we've described is not magic. It is applied network science — algorithms that have powered web search and social networks for decades, repurposed for a new domain. The magic, if there is any, lies in the application. In the willingness to take public information seriously. In the patience to let patterns emerge from noise.

The follow graph is a map of a living, breathing ecosystem. It changes every day. New nodes appear. Old edges dissolve. Clusters form and reform. Attention flows like water, always seeking the lowest ground — which is to say, the most interesting ground.

Our job is to watch. To map. To see.

Because attention is all you need.
